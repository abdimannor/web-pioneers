Internet protocols

Vint Cerf

Vint Cerf helped shape TCP/IP, the family of protocols that made it possible for different networks to be joined without becoming one central system.

His importance lies in the architecture: the internet was not to be a place, a computer or a company, but a way for many independent networks to understand each other.

Read more about Vint Cerf (Swedish Wikipedia)

Why he matters

He helped the internet grow without becoming centrally controlled.

Vint Cerf's work revolved around a basic problem: how do you get separate networks, built with different technology and for different purposes, to communicate reliably?

The answer was not that everyone should use the same hardware or the same local network. Instead, shared rules were needed for how information is split up, addressed, sent and put back together. That is an important reason why the internet could grow organically.

1943

Born in the USA

Vinton Gray Cerf was born in Connecticut and later became a central figure behind the technical foundation of the internet.

1970s

The work with Bob Kahn

Cerf and Kahn developed the ideas behind TCP/IP, the basis of the internet's communication model.

1983

TCP/IP becomes the standard

When ARPANET moved over to TCP/IP, the model became a practical foundation for the internet that then grew.

1990s

The internet goes public

The open nature of the protocols meant that more networks, universities, companies and users could connect.

2000s

Continued internet work

Cerf kept working on the internet's development, standards, accessibility and long-term responsibility.

Principle

A network of networks

His work shows why the internet works best when different systems can meet through open rules.

Building blocks

The internet needed rules for movement, not just connections.

TCP

Splits up data, keeps track of delivery and makes communication more reliable.

IP

Gives packets addresses and makes it possible for them to find routes between different networks.

Packet switching

Information is sent in smaller parts that can take different routes and still be put back together.

Open interconnection

New networks can connect without the whole system having to be rebuilt from scratch.

One message, many routes.

The message is split into numbered packets. Each packet is sent on its own, and the routers can send them along different routes. That is why they can arrive in the wrong order. At the receiver, TCP uses the numbers to put the message together in the right order. (In reality TCP's sequence numbers count bytes, not whole packets.)

    Glossary

    Technical terms on this page

    TCP/IP
    The family of protocols the internet is built on. IP gives data packets addresses and routes them between networks; TCP keeps track of delivery so that data arrives complete and in the right order.
    Packet switching
    Data is split into small packets that are sent separately, can take different routes through the network and are put back together at the receiver.
